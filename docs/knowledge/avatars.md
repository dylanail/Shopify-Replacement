# Avatars

## Five categories that describe a person

1. **Demographics** — age, sex, location, income, family
2. **Experiences** — what has happened to them (a failed product, a diagnosis, a move, a wedding)
3. **Behaviours** — what they do (train five days a week, scroll at night, buy on Amazon first)
4. **Emotions** — what they feel about the problem (embarrassment, frustration, fear)
5. **Desires** — which instinct the product serves for them and how badly

## The core avatar is desire-based

One core avatar per product, defined by the desire it satisfies: "people who
want to stop feeling old when they stand up" rather than "women 45–60". The
core avatar is who the product is for at the level of the instinct; it stays
stable across years and products, which is why operators who stick to one or
two avatars compound (RXBAR → David Protein; pet brands built on the same
owner).

## Sub-avatars layer the other four categories

A sub-avatar is the core avatar plus a demographic, an experience, a
behaviour or an emotion. Each sub-avatar produces an **angle**: the reason to
buy stated in that person's terms. "New mothers whose back hurts from
carrying" and "desk workers whose back hurts from sitting" share the core
desire and need different hooks, different imagery and different proof.

Give every avatar a **label** the buyer would use for themselves; it goes in
the ad ("parenting must-have", "for the last-minute kind of person").

## Scale tiers

| Tier | Monthly reach the avatar can support | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Niche | up to 100k | Fine for organic and small tests; a brand cannot scale on it alone |
| Mid | 100k–1M | A sub-avatar that can carry a campaign |
| Mass | 1M+ | The core avatar of a scalable brand |

## The questions the builder asks

When the platform builds a store it asks these in order and accepts
"I don't know" for every one; a blank is filled from research and marked as
a guess to be confirmed.

1. Who is the person that buys this, in one sentence they would agree with?
2. Which instinct is it really about? (health, status, sex, comfort, control, belonging)
3. What did they try before this and why did it fail?
4. What do they say when it works? (the outcome in their words)
5. What are they afraid of getting wrong?
6. Where do they find out about things like this?
7. What does it cost them per month to not solve this?
8. Who else is selling to them, and what are those pages saying?

## Belief building

A page or an ad moves someone from "I do not believe it" to "I believe it for
me" with **visible proof**: the mechanism shown working, a before/after, a
comparison against what they were going to buy, people like them saying it
worked, the guarantee that removes the risk of being wrong. Claims without
visible proof read as stage-2 shouting.

## From the avatar training

- An avatar is "a group of specific people most likely to buy"; the goal is
  relatability, because relatability earns trust and trust buys. Targeting
  is done by the creative, not by clicking buttons.
- Build order: desires → experiences → emotions → behaviours → demographics.
  Demographics are last and usually implied.
- **Checkers**: a desire can be said as "I want X / I need Y"; an experience
  is a circumstance or event with the emotion removed (product experiences
  always state an outcome); an emotion passes the "I feel" test and a
  secondary emotion (frustrated, overwhelmed) means go deeper with why and
  what; a behaviour is what they do and how often; a belief is unpacked into
  what happened, how it feels and what they do because of it.
- One desire per avatar. The core avatar is one category, isolated; a
  sub-avatar layers more. The more categories, the more specific.
- Broader avatars need a better product, offer, brand and skill. Specific
  but not alienating avatars forgive weaker execution.
- Stage 1 (under $100k/month): one desire-based core avatar; sub-avatar,
  sub-avatar, sub-avatar for 2–4 weeks before switching desire. Above
  $500k/month: two or three core avatars. $1M–$10M: three to ten with
  branding for new identities.
- Angles come from sub-avatars: "a harness that gives confidence" and "a
  harness that gives control" share a desire and differ by experience and
  emotion.
- Labels call the sub-avatar out in the hook: "POV: you work the night shift".

## The product overview document

1. Name and price (with the compare-at).
2. How it works, then a sixth-grade rewrite.
3. Features (materials, components, mechanisms).
4. Assumed benefits: for each feature, why does this matter?
5. Assumed desires: for each benefit, why does someone want that?
6. Assumed new mechanisms.
7. Hidden mechanisms: what it does that is not advertised; what it includes
   that competitors do not; if it worked too well what would people complain
   about; how a friend would describe it.

Everything a model writes here is assumed until research confirms it.
